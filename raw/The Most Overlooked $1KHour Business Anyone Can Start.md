**What You're Selling:** You're running shop class for kids ages 7 to 18. You show up with folding tables and hand tools, the kids build something simple like a wooden robot or a bird feeder, and they take it home. Most schools dropped shop class years ago, so parents have nowhere else to go for this. Mike hosted a father-son build last December, and *only two of the 38 dads had ever touched a power tool*.

It took Mike a while to figure out that parents are buying confidence for their kids, and woodworking is just how he delivers it. (As a dad of four, I can tell you that's absolutely something I'd pay for.)

**Step 1: Write your Post**

Post a static image to your personal Facebook page, then to local homeschool groups and moms groups. Mike's wording is basically this: *"Woodworking shop class for kids to build confidence. Message me for details."*

Leave the price out so parents have to message you. Mike says they don't read websites anyway and would rather ask you directly. Expect four questions: how old, how much, when, and where. Have your answers ready before you post.

Homeschool groups are the best place to start because those moms all talk to each other and they are always looking for learning opportunities for the kids. Also, many programs have budgets to pay for classes like these. Mike is in three groups, one with 9,000 members, and he has taught about 500 students so far. Half of them are repeat customers.

**Step 2: Run Two Free Classes**

Mike's first two classes were free, held in backyards with five or six kids each. You're doing these for the photos and reviews, since you have no social proof yet. One of those free classes led directly to that father-son event, which was 38 pairs at $150 each in his second month. Have every parent sign a waiver that includes photo permission, then post pictures every day.

**Step 3: The “Starter Kit”**

About $425 covers six kids at one table:

- A 6-foot folding table with a half-inch plywood top clamped on
- Two miter boxes from Harbor Freight
- Six tape measures with the fractions printed on them
- Six speed squares
- Screwdrivers, clamps, and a few sanding blocks

If you borrow a table and use the screwdrivers you already own, you can easily get under $300. Buy cheap hand tools, (think Harbor Freight and FB Marketplace) because kids will drop and break them. **Collect full payment up front through a Square or Stripe link, and you can use that money to buy the kit.** Mike pays about $95 a month for regular business insurance.

Skip the power tools in the beginning. A set for six older kids runs about $4,100, and Mike has taught whole classes where the kids were perfectly happy with handsaws.

When Mike needed tools for his trade school, (separate from these kid classes) he cold messaged a Milwaukee employee on LinkedIn. He pointed out that a tradesperson spends $30,000 to $45,000 on tools over a career and asked if Milwaukee wanted to reach those customers at 18. They've sent him about $5,000 in free gear so far. Never hurts to ask.

**Step 4: Price per Kid, per Hour**

Mike charges $75 an hour per kid for ages 7 to 9, because they need more supervision, and $50 an hour for ages 10 and up. A one-hour class with ten little ones brings in $750. Materials run about $150, so **you keep $600.**

He doesn't discount for typical groups of 8 to 12 kids, because he has no competition. For school breaks he runs morning camps from 9 to noon, priced at $300 for three days and $500 for five. ***Spring break alone cleared $12,000 in one week of teaching mornings.***

**Step 5: Keep the Projects Simple**

Mike has about ten projects and says that's enough to stay busy for five years. He buys plans on Etsy, and his American flag plan cost $9. Kids love making totes, flags, bird feeders, and cornhole boards.

A few rules he learned the hard way:

- Use screws instead of nails, and buy sheetrock screws by the bucket.
- Buy knot-free pine so the kids can skip paint and stain and still finish on time.
- Prep the harder cuts yourself before class.
- Bring a bin of scrap wood for kids who finish early.

Safety is where you can't bend. Eye protection goes on before anyone touches the table, and Mike opens every class with the shop rules. He hasn't had a single injury.

**Step 6: Go After Group Contracts**

Once you have photos and reviews, pitch homeschool co-ops and private schools. Mike's $30,000 contract with a homeschool co-op covers 40 students for two hours every Friday over 14 weeks. He brings his family plus two helpers at $150 each, everything fits in one pickup truck, and he'll clear about $10,000 after buying the extra tools.

Also **check whether your state has a school choice scholarship program.** Mike is an approved vendor with Florida's Step Up program, so those parents pay nothing out of pocket, and the state pays him within 24 hours.

**What This Can Make:** Mike's estimate for someone doing this part time and fully mobile is $1,500 on a Saturday and another $600 on a Sunday afternoon. At two weekends a month, that's about $4,000 a month while keeping your full-time job.

His $50,600 stretch came after he moved classes into a commercial space, which he says made moms trust him more. Everything before that ran off a folding table in someone's backyard. A single one-hour class of ten younger kids brings in $750, which pays for your entire kit and materials on day one.
