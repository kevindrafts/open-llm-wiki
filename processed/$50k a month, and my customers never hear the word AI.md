## Why you should sell booked jobs, not software.

Hey Boring Marketers,

You'd think the hardest part of an AI business is the AI. Last week's call convinced me it's the opposite.

Two of the best builders in the community showed up, both with something real. Marcus built his own software to run a small business and used it to save a mobile detailer weeks from closing. Rey, out of academia, built a tool that maps your workflows and finds opportunities you're missing.

Neither was stuck on the tech. Both were stuck selling it. The moment Marcus mentions AI to a local owner, their eyes glaze over. Rey is invisible: no following, no idea how to pitch what he made.

I kept telling them the same thing. The owner doesn't care about AI. They want the phone to ring and their week booked. My diesel company runs on exactly that, and it's doing around $50k a month without a customer ever hearing the word AI. Sell the result, and keep the software out of the conversation.

## Hot seat #1: Marcus and the detailer that almost closed

Marcus Cousin got tired of paying for Shopify plus every add-on it takes to run a store, so he built his own replacement. He calls it Run1T: one platform for the website, booking, email, and reporting, with an AI layer so the owner can ask it questions instead of clicking through dashboards. Ask for the last 30 days and it returns appointments booked, amount invoiced, average job value, and where the jobs came from. No customer data exposed.

His first client was a Florida mobile detailer about to shut down. They let him in because they had nothing to lose. Three months later they'd tripled their volume, added a second vehicle, and booked more work in a quarter than in the whole first half of the year. Last 30 days: 39 appointments at a $163 average.

Run1T's 30-day readout for the same business: 39 booked jobs at a $163 average.

He's charging them $80 a month. His question: how do I sell this to other businesses without it sounding like an AI pitch, and what do I charge?

This is the most common trap I see with people who can build. They price the software instead of the outcome. Marcus took a business from nearly closed to triple the volume, and he's charging like it's an $80 SaaS tool. Here's what I told him:

**a) Charge per result, not a flat fee.** Marcus tripled their volume and charges $80 a month, less than one $163 job. I'd drop the flat fee and charge per booked job, say $15. At 20 jobs a month that's $400, all paid out of money he made them.

**b) Make it a performance deal.** "You pay when it works, that's how confident I am" beats a monthly software bill every time. It's Hormozi's hard-to-refuse offer: nothing for the owner to lose, so it spreads fast.

**c) Move up the ticket.** Most detailers are small and price sensitive, so I pointed him at the bigger tickets in the same world: ceramic coating, paint protection film, wraps, scratch and dent repair. Higher ticket, fewer jobs needed for the return to be obvious.

**d) Lead with a free assessment, not a subscription.** His platform already reads a business's data, so that's the door opener: "I just tripled a detailer's jobs and I can find the low-hanging fruit in your marketing, want a free assessment?" It opens into the performance deal. Get the first 25 clients by hand before touching ads.

Then the bigger version, the one my own business runs on. Don't sell software to detailers, become the detailing brand. Own the marketing, the rankings, the phone, and the dispatch, and send the jobs to operators who fulfill them. That's Diesel Dudes. I get the phone ringing across a region, then pay independent mechanics to do the work. It does about $50k a month and ranks first on Google for "mobile diesel mechanic" in Columbia, second in Rock Hill, ahead of every direct competitor. None of those customers care that AI is behind it. They wanted their truck fixed. Own the racetrack and it's easy to tell an operator, "I have more calls than I can handle, can I send them to you?"

Marcus already has the hard part: a real case study, working tech, and a niche. MCP servers are hard to build right, and he did it. What's left is to go have the conversations and find the win-win.

## Hot seat #2: Rey, the scientist who couldn't get seen

Rey built a free, open-source tool called Workflow Router. Feed it the tools and assets you already have and it maps your workflows as a graph, then surfaces business paths hiding in resources you're already paying for. His background is unusual: an academic, a historian of science, whose real love is the cultural analysis of social feeds, reading what people say and what it means.

His problem had nothing to do with any of that. In his words, he's basically obscure: almost no following, no marketing instinct, no idea which angle to lead with. Sharp enough to build six apps, stuck at getting anyone to care.

![Rey's Workflow Router graph mapping tools, datasets, and steps](https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80/uploads/asset/file/2c6ce505-3d2e-4e35-87d0-cf26b5af617f/rey-workflow-router.png?t=1789747168)

Rey's Workflow Router maps every tool, dataset, and step as one graph.

The thing holding most technical people back isn't the build, it's that they treat distribution as someone else's problem. Rey has six working apps and no one to show them to. Here's how I'd fix that:

**a) His background is the brand.** Rey shouldn't hide the professor. Lead with it: the scientist who turns marketing into something measurable. Lab coat, "local marketing scientist." In a market where everyone sounds the same, that's what makes him stand out.

**b) "Too niche" is a distribution problem, not a demand problem.** Rey thinks his cultural-analysis work is too specialized to sell. It isn't. Brands spend real money on voice of customer, social listening, and trend spotting, and as AMA pointed out, the qualitative read is the part AI can't do, since AI only surfaces what it already knows. He shouldn't chase brands directly. Better to go through agencies that already hold the relationships and bring them a specialist they don't have. Get listed where they look: LinkedIn, Substack, and marketplaces like Catalant, MarketerHire, or Toptal.

**c) If he goes the services route, niche down and go local.** Point a keyword tool at a local market and find a supply-demand gap, ideally a $1,000-plus ticket so the math works on few jobs. Everyone chases plumbers and HVAC; scan a dozen mid-size cities and something like garage remodeling or driveway resurfacing may be wide open. Then the pitch is plain: "I help driveway resurfacing companies book more jobs with fewer headaches." The AI stays behind the curtain.

**d) His real blocker is false productivity.** I fight it myself, every day. Talking to AI feels like progress, but the move that matters is a conversation with a business owner, and AI is a comfortable way to avoid it. I told Rey to stop chasing the perfect price or tool and go get one case study, even 15 or 20 hours of free work, because that story wins the next customer. As he put it, he needed a person to tell him this, not a machine.

Rey already has the rare part: deep expertise, tools built, a background almost nobody in the space can claim. He's missing a narrow niche and a first story. Same gap as Marcus, different starting point.

## The pattern across both

Two very different builders, one identical gap. Marcus and Rey both had the product finished and the distribution barely started. That's the pattern in almost every hot seat I run. The tech is rarely the hard part. Picking a niche, making an offer that's hard to refuse, and actually talking to a business owner is. If there's one thing to take from both of these, it's that the build feels like the work, but the job only moves when you go have the conversation.
