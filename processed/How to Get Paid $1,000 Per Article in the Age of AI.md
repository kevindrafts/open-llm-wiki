**What the Business Actually Is**

B2B software companies need long-form blog content, *usually 2,500 to 3,000 words per article*, to show up in Google and increasingly inside AI tools like ChatGPT and Claude. The assignments sound like "Claude Code vs. Cursor," "8 Alternatives to QuickBase," or a step-by-step tutorial for building a specific automated workflow. The company typically hands you the keywords they want to target, so you don't need to be an SEO expert going in. You just need to know the basics, like how to structure headings and keywords.

What they're really paying for is someone who has actually ***used*** the tools. Steph spends most of those five or six hours signing up for the software she's writing about, clicking around, grabbing screenshots, and reading Reddit threads to see what real users are complaining about. If people are griping about confusing pricing, she goes and figures out the pricing herself. **A chatbot can't fake that kind of firsthand research, and it's why these companies will pay four figures for a single article.**

**Step 1: Pick a Niche**

Before our conversation, Steph hired a handful of freelance writers on Upwork (each making over $3,000 a month) for 20-minute calls about how newcomers break in. **None of them had a writing degree.** What they all agreed on was that your first month should be spent figuring out what kind of writing you want to do: book editing, website copy, ads, scripts, email, SEO blog posts, etc...

Start as a specialist. Steph began as a generalist and said it took her a while to realize people hire the writer who's obviously great at one thing. Pick something you already have a glimmer of interest in. If you love Texas barbecue, go after barbecue brands and e-commerce companies selling grills and rubs. If you tinker with AI tools on the weekend, target SaaS companies.

**Step 2: Create a Portfolio Before Anyone Hires You**

This is one of the rare businesses where you can solve the chicken-and-egg problem by yourself. You need four or five samples that show range, something like three blog posts and a landing page. Nobody has to pay you for them. Write an article about what makes Franklin Barbecue different, or a comparison of two AI tools you actually use, and put them on a simple site built with Hostinger or Replit. Who's stopping you? No one.

**Step 3: Find Clients on Upwork and Through Cold Pitches**

Ten years in, Upwork is still where Steph finds a lot of her work. The leads are sitting right there waiting for you. When she's actively looking, she applies to about ten jobs a day, choosing ones where she can explain exactly how she'd help that specific person and where one blog post could turn into an ongoing contract.

Outside of Upwork, learn the cold pitch. Keep it to four or five sentences. Introduce yourself, mention something specific you genuinely like about their company, link your portfolio, and say you'd love to connect. Steph tried a new beef protein powder, emailed the founder to tell him the flavor was awesome, asked in that same message whether he needed a blog writer, and got hired. Another time she listened to an entire podcast episode from an agency before pitching them, referenced a specific detail, and they called her right away. ***That extra homework is what gets you noticed.***

**Step 4: Price by the Project**

Starting out, $30-$40 an hour is a realistic range on Upwork, especially with a solid portfolio and a tailored pitch. Steph worked cheap in the beginning too. One of those early clients turned out to be Biggie Smalls's son, who was launching a CBD brand, and that relationship grew into press releases and PR work she'd never done before. ***Take the reps early and raise your rate as trust builds.***

Once you have some experience, switch to flat project quotes. Before quoting, ask roughly how many pages or words the job involves and whether they have existing copy you can look at, since rough copy takes longer to fix and no copy means starting from scratch. You can still do the math off an hourly rate in your head, but give the client one number so nobody gets surprised. If they're okay with it, run a note-taker like Granola on your kickoff call so you can pull up deadlines and details later.

**Step 5: Use AI Where It Helps Most**

The biggest mistake Steph sees is writers trying to automate the entire process. They spend hours configuring tools, the output still isn't right, and they burn out. At Zapier, she copy edited a report on how companies use AI successfully, and AI handled only about 18% of the workflow, usually a step or two with a human reviewing the result.

She uses Gemini for processing video and audio, Claude for marketing content, and ChatGPT for research. For tutorials, she screen-records herself building the workflow while narrating her thoughts out loud, then has AI turn that recording into a first draft using her own template. The ideas and structure are hers, and she estimates 70% to 80% of the final words are hers too.

**Step 6: Expand Every Account**

***Once a company trusts you, they really don't want to go find another writer. Look for ways to do more for them.*** Offer to upload the posts yourself, pitch a series instead of a single article, or pick up their landing pages. Steph has turned one-off projects into contracts that lasted years.

**The Math:**

At $1,000 per article and five to six hours of work, you're earning roughly $165 to $200 an hour. Five articles a month is $5,000. Ten articles is $10,000, and that's still only about 60 hours of work spread across the month. Add a side offer on top, like Steph's college essay coaching at $450 per five-hour package, and you've got a real income with almost no startup cost beyond a basic website.

Spend your first month picking a niche and writing your samples. In month two, apply to ten Upwork jobs a day and send a few cold pitches every week. Even if your first client pays $300 an article, you're building the portfolio and relationships that get you to $1,000.
