Mobile enrichment classes sell a structured learning experience through local parent networks, repeat bookings, and school or homeschool contracts. Chris Koerner's October 8, 2026 newsletter profiles Mike Herod's children's woodworking business in West Palm Beach. The case connects simple equipment and repeatable projects with parent trust, but its revenue claims do not establish typical earnings or profit per hour.

# Mobile enrichment class businesses

## The offer and first customers

Herod teaches children ages 7 to 18 to build projects they can take home. The source describes confidence as the benefit parents buy, with woodworking as the activity that delivers it. Bird feeders, totes, flags, and cornhole boards give families a visible result.

The reported acquisition sequence starts with a personal Facebook post, then local homeschool and parent groups. Herod leaves the price out of the post and answers questions about age, price, timing, and location in messages. This differs from the price-visible ads in [[local-service-brands-and-partner-fulfillment]]; each case describes one operator's approach, without testing which converts better.

His first two classes were free backyard sessions for five or six children, used to collect photos and reviews. One reportedly led to a father-son event with 38 pairs paying $150 each, or $5,700 in gross sales. The newsletter reports about 500 students taught and half returning, without defining the measurement period or repeat-customer calculation.

## Repeatable delivery

The source describes a roughly $425 hand-tool kit for six children, including a folding table with a plywood worktop, miter boxes, tape measures, squares, screwdrivers, clamps, and sanding blocks. That figure is equipment cost, not a complete startup budget. Herod reportedly pays about $95 monthly for business insurance and collects payment before classes through Square or Stripe.

He keeps a library of about ten projects, buys some plans on Etsy, prepares difficult cuts before class, and brings scrap wood for early finishers. A limited project library can reduce preparation and purchasing complexity while leaving room for repeat visits. Advance payment resembles the customer-funded materials pattern in [[offline-local-arbitrage-businesses]].

The clipping describes eye protection, opening shop rules, and parental waivers with photo permission. It reports no injuries, but supplies no independent safety assessment or evidence that the described arrangements suit every age or group size. These are details of Herod's operation, not a complete operating standard.

## Reported prices and economics

| Example | Source figures | What the numbers establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Younger children's class | $75 per child per hour for ages 7-9; ten children generate $750, with about $150 materials | $600 remains before labor, preparation, travel, insurance, venue, and other costs |
| Older children's class | $50 per child per hour for ages 10 and up | A reported price, without attendance or cost data |
| Morning camps | $300 for three days or $500 for five, 9 a.m. to noon | Package prices; the claimed $12,000 spring-break result lacks a full cost breakdown |
| Homeschool co-op | $30,000 for 40 students, two hours each Friday for 14 weeks | 28 scheduled class hours and about $1,071 contract revenue per class hour, before costs and additional working time |

For the co-op contract, Herod reportedly brings family members and two helpers at $150 each. The source does not state the helpers' pay period clearly. It says he expects about $10,000 left after buying additional tools, without complete accounts or a valuation of family labor. The headline's $1,000-per-hour framing therefore cannot be read as the operator's hourly take-home pay.

Koerner also reports $50,600 brought in between March and July after Herod moved into commercial premises. That is evidence about a business with a venue, not solely the mobile setup promoted in the article. The suggested weekend schedule totals $4,200 across two weekends at $1,500 Saturday plus $600 Sunday; the source rounds this to about $4,000 and does not reconcile all expenses.

## Group contracts and ideas to test

The expansion path runs from small parent-paid classes to camps, family events, and homeschool or private-school contracts. Photos, reviews, and repeat attendance give an organizer evidence of delivery before committing a larger group. The newsletter also reports that Herod was an approved Florida Step Up vendor with rapid payment. That is a dated account of his arrangement, not verified current eligibility or payment guidance.

A possible software opportunity is a small booking and operations tool for independent enrichment providers, covering parent inquiries, age groups, payments, project history, and group scheduling. This is an inference from the workflow, not evidence that providers need another product. Repeated administrative problems in paid classes would be stronger validation than the newsletter's earnings headline. See [[ai-assisted-market-research-and-validation]].
